\textbf{In one paragraph.} Every descriptor below is a single number computed from one file, the
VASP \textsf{CHGCAR}: the crystal structure in its header and the electron density $\rho$ on a periodic
grid. The descriptors fall into three groups. \emph{Bonding} descriptors (18) ask how the charge is
arranged around each atom and in the bonds. \emph{Structural} descriptors (18) look at the density
of the whole cell: how well it connects, its peaks and valleys, its directionality and its
information content. \emph{Heterogeneity} descriptors (18) ask how different the atoms of one

supercell) and unchanged by translating or rotating the crystal; the few exceptions to the letter
of this rule are stated in the entries concerned.}}

\section{What a CHGCAR contains, and the common machinery}

\subsection{The density in a CHGCAR}
VASP uses the projector-augmented-wave (PAW) method~\cite{blochl}. The density written to the
CHGCAR is the \emph{valence pseudo-density}: it contains only the electrons the POTCAR treats as
valence (so semicore $s$, $p$ or $d$ states count when the POTCAR includes them, e.g.\ the
\texttt{\_pv} and \texttt{\_sv} variants), and inside the augmentation sphere of each atom the true,
nodal valence density is replaced by a smooth one. Three consequences run through this document:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=0.2ex,topsep=0.3ex]
\item The deep core electrons are absent. Quantities that in an all-electron density are dominated
  by the core (the nuclear cusp, the atomic shell structure of the Laplacian) here describe the
  valence electrons only.
\item Inside the augmentation spheres the density is smooth and can even be slightly negative.
  It need not peak at the nucleus: the pseudo-density of many $s$/$p$ elements peaks on a shell or
  in the bonds.
\item The numbers depend somewhat on the POTCAR chosen, because it fixes the number of valence
  electrons. Densities of one dataset should come from one consistent set of POTCARs.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Notation}
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.56\linewidth}
\vspace{0pt}
\textbf{Grid.} The cell (volume $V$) is divided into $N_v$ voxels $k$ of volume $\Delta V = V/N_v$. $\rho_k$ is the
density at voxel $k$ in e/\AA$^3$ (the CHGCAR value divided by $V$). The total charge is
$Q_{\rm tot}=\sum_k \rho_k\,\Delta V$, and $\langle\rho\rangle = Q_{\rm tot}/V$ is the mean density.
A sum $\sum_k$ runs over all voxels; it approximates an integral, $\sum_k f_k\Delta V\approx\int f\,dV$.

\textbf{Nearest nucleus.} For each voxel, $i(k)$ is the nearest atom (periodic images included),
$r_k$ its distance and $\hat u_k$ the unit vector from that nucleus to the voxel.

\textbf{Regions.} Around every nucleus $i$ lie a core sphere of radius $aR_i$ and an outer
sphere of radius $bR_i$, with $R_i$ the covalent radius of the atom~\cite{cordero} and $a=0.79$,
$b=1.14$. A voxel is core if it lies in any core sphere, bond if it lies in an outer sphere but in no
core sphere, and interstitial if it lies outside every outer sphere. With
$s_k=\min_i r_{ik}/R_i$, the distance to the nearest nucleus in units of that atom's covalent
radius:
\[
\begin{array}{ll}
\text{core:} & s_k \le a\\
\text{bond:} & a < s_k \le b\\
\text{interstitial:} & s_k > b
\end{array}
\]

\medskip
The spheres scale with the size of each atom, so the regions mean the same thing for H and for
Cs. A bond of length $d$ between atoms $A$ and $B$ has $d\approx R_A+R_B$. The two factors are the
1st and 99th percentiles of $d/(R_A+R_B)$ over the nearest neighbour of every atom of a
6,059-structure dataset (median 0.95): the core spheres of bonded atoms overlap for fewer than 1\%
of the atoms, and the outer spheres cover the whole bond line for all but 1\%. The regions are
found by assigning every voxel to the atom with the smallest $r_{ik}/R_i$ and testing $r_{ik}$
against $aR_i$ and $bR_i$ of that atom.

\textbf{Derivatives.} The gradient $\nabla\rho$, the Laplacian $\nabla^2\rho$ and the Hessian
$H_{ab}=\partial_a\partial_b\rho$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_1\le\lambda_2\le\lambda_3$ are computed
spectrally (FFT) on the periodic, possibly non-orthogonal grid, with the full metric of the cell.
For a plane-wave density this is exact up to round-off. Quantities that are defined in atomic units
(the NCI thresholds and the information measures) are converted to bohr and e/bohr$^3$ first
($1$\,e/bohr$^3$ $=6.748$\,e/\AA$^3$).

\textbf{Degenerate cases.} A descriptor that is undefined for a special input (a perfectly uniform
density, an empty region, a structure without bonds) returns a documented constant, and a flag is
set in the metadata. No descriptor returns NaN.

\textbf{Numerical stability.} Candidate descriptors whose value changed by more than about 10\% on
the dataset when the derivatives were taken by finite differences instead of FFT, or when the grid
was coarsened to 80\%, were dropped (Sec.~\ref{sec:dropped}).

\section{Bonding descriptors (18)}

\subsection{Where the charge sits: radial moments and region fractions}
The simplest picture of a solid is a set of atoms. These six descriptors ask how far the charge
lies from its nearest nucleus, and how it is shared between the core, the bond region and the space
in between. They are moments of the \emph{radial distribution} of the charge,
$P(r)=\frac{1}{Q_{\rm tot}}\sum_k\rho_k\,\Delta V\,\delta(r-r_k)$, the probability that an electron
is at distance $r$ from the nucleus it is closest to.

\desc{m1}{m1}{\AA\quad$\ge 0$}
\Def The mean distance between an electron and its nearest nucleus, averaged over all electrons in
the cell: every voxel is weighted by the charge it holds.
\Frm
\[
m_1=\frac{\sumk\rho_k\,r_k}{\sumk\rho_k}=\int_0^\infty r\,P(r)\,dr .
\]
\Phy $m_1$ is a charge-weighted radius of the atoms in the crystal. For a single exponential
(Slater 1s-type) density $\rho\propto e^{-2\zeta r}$ it equals $3/(2\zeta)$, so it grows as the
valence charge becomes more diffuse (smaller orbital exponent $\zeta$). In a solid the distance to
the nearest nucleus cannot exceed the size of that atom's Voronoi cell, so $m_1$ also carries the
packing: a density spread evenly over a sphere of radius $R$ would give $m_1=3R/4$. Small values
mean compact, tightly bound valence charge (first-row elements, compact $d$ and $f$ shells, short
bonds); large values mean diffuse valence charge and a large volume per atom (alkali and
alkaline-earth metals, open structures). Large $m_1$ usually goes with soft, polarizable atoms.
\Cav Only valence electrons are counted, so $m_1$ is a valence radius, not an all-electron one.
Adding semicore states to the valence (another POTCAR) lowers it.

\desc{m2}{m2}{\AA$^2$\quad$\ge 0$}
\Def The mean \emph{squared} distance between an electron and its nearest nucleus.
\Frm
\[
m_2=\frac{\sumk\rho_k\,r_k^2}{\sumk\rho_k}=\int_0^\infty r^2\,P(r)\,dr .
\]
\Phy Because $r^2$ grows faster than $r$, $m_2$ weights the outer charge more strongly than $m_1$. It is the
radial ``moment of inertia'' of the charge about the nuclei. For the Slater density
$m_2=3/\zeta^2$. The same moment $\langle r^2\rangle$ sets the Langevin diamagnetic susceptibility
of closed-shell atoms and ions, $\chi_{\rm dia}=-\mu_0 n e^2\langle r^2\rangle/(6m_e)$, per $n$
atoms per volume~\cite{ashcroft}. So $m_2$ measures how much charge sits in the outer,
easily deformed region of the atoms.

\desc{sigma_r2}{sigma_r2}{\AA$^2$\quad$\ge 0$}
\Def The variance of the electron--nucleus distance: how broad the radial distribution of the
charge is about its mean.
\Frm
\[
\sigma_r^2 = m_2 - m_1^2 = \frac{\sumk\rho_k\,(r_k-m_1)^2}{\sumk\rho_k}.
\]
\Phy $\sigma_r^2$ separates the \emph{width} of the radial distribution from its \emph{position}
($m_1$). For a single exponential $\sigma_r^2=3/(4\zeta^2)$, so $\sigma_r^2/m_1^2=1/3$ whatever
$\zeta$ is. A ratio $\sigma_r^2/m_1^2$ well above $1/3$ therefore points to a distribution that is
broader than a single shell: charge both close to the nuclei (compact $d$, $f$ or semicore states)
and far from them (diffuse $s$/$p$ valence, interstitial charge). Narrow distributions belong to
crystals whose valence charge occupies one well-defined shell.

\desc{f_core}{f_core}{dimensionless\quad$[0,1]$}
\Def The fraction of the electrons in the cell that lie inside a core sphere, within $0.79R_i$ of
some nucleus $i$.
\Frm
\[
f_{\rm core}=\frac{\sum_{k:\,s_k\le a}\rho_k}{\sumk\rho_k}.
\]
\Phy The charge held tightly by the nuclei. It is high for elements whose valence (as the POTCAR
defines it) is compact: small electronegative atoms (O, F, N), the $3d$ and $4f$ shells of
transition metals and rare earths, and semicore $p$ states. It is low for diffuse $s$/$p$ valence
charge. $f_{\rm core}$ is not the chemist's core: it is the share of the \emph{valence} charge that
stays within about four fifths of each atom's covalent radius.
\Cav Depends on the POTCAR choice: semicore electrons treated as valence raise it.

\desc{f_bond}{f_bond}{dimensionless\quad$[0,1]$}
\Def The fraction of the electrons in the bond region: inside an outer sphere ($1.14R_i$) but in no
core sphere.
\Frm
\[
f_{\rm bond}=\frac{\sum_{k:\,a<s_k\le b}\rho_k}{\sumk\rho_k}.
\]
\Phy The outer spheres of two bonded atoms meet on the bond line, so the region holds the bonds
and the outer part of the valence shells, and $f_{\rm bond}$ is the share of the charge in the
bonding region. It is
large for covalent and moderately packed crystals, where the valence charge is drawn into the bonds.
It is small when the charge is either kept on the atoms (large $f_{\rm core}$) or spread into the
interstitial (large $f_{\rm int}$).

\desc{f_int}{f_int}{dimensionless\quad$[0,1]$}
\Def The fraction of the electrons outside every outer sphere ($s_k>1.14$).
\Frm
\[
f_{\rm int}=\frac{\sum_{k:\,s_k>b}\rho_k}{\sumk\rho_k},\qquad f_{\rm core}+f_{\rm bond}+f_{\rm int}=1 .
\]
\Phy The charge in the space between the atoms, beyond the reach of every atom's own bonds: the
open voids of frameworks, the diffuse valence charge of metals, lone pairs pointing into voids.
Since the spheres scale with the atoms, a large atom does not by itself raise $f_{\rm int}$. It is
large in open frameworks (SiC 25\%, BPO$_4$ 22\%, NaCl 12\%) and small in dense hydrides
(TiH$_2$ 1\%).

\subsection{Is the charge spherical? Gradient anisotropy}
Around an isolated, spherical atom the density gradient points straight at the nucleus. Overlap
with neighbours, bonds, lone pairs and aspherical $d$ occupations all tilt it sideways.

\desc{zeta}{zeta}{dimensionless\quad$[0,1]$}
\Def The share of the density gradient that does \emph{not} point along the line to the nearest
nucleus, averaged over the cell with weight $|\nabla\rho|$.
\Frm With $\theta_k$ the angle between $\nabla\rho_k$ and $\hat u_k$,
\[
\zeta = 1-\frac{\sumk|\nabla\rho_k\cdot\hat u_k|}{\sumk|\nabla\rho_k|}
      = 1-\frac{\sumk|\nabla\rho_k|\,|\cos\theta_k|}{\sumk|\nabla\rho_k|}.
\]
\Phy $\zeta=0$ for spherical atoms that do not overlap: the gradient is radial everywhere. If the
gradient directions were random with respect to the nucleus, $\langle|\cos\theta|\rangle=1/2$ and
$\zeta=1/2$. Two effects make $\zeta$ positive: (i) \emph{overlap}: between two atoms the gradient is
the sum of two radial fields pointing at different nuclei, so it is not radial with respect to
either; (ii) \emph{deformation}: bonds, lone pairs and non-spherical orbital occupations. $\zeta$
is thus one number for how far the crystal's density departs from a superposition of isolated
spherical atoms, the information a deformation density $\rho-\sum\rho_{\rm atom}$ conveys, but
without reference atoms. Because of the weight $|\nabla\rho|$ it is dominated by the steep outer
parts of the atoms rather than by the flat interstitial. For most crystals it is a few per cent.
\Lim Uniform density ($\nabla\rho=0$): 0, flagged.

\subsection{Where charge is concentrated: the Laplacian}
The Laplacian compares the density at a point with its surroundings: averaged over a small sphere
of radius $h$, $\langle\rho\rangle_{\rm sphere}-\rho(\mathbf r)=\tfrac{h^2}{6}\nabla^2\rho+O(h^4)$.
Where $\nabla^2\rho<0$ the density is higher than around it, \emph{locally concentrated}, as in
atomic shells, covalent bonds and lone pairs. Where $\nabla^2\rho>0$ it is depleted. In the theory
of atoms in molecules the sign also tells which energy dominates: by the local virial theorem
$\tfrac14\nabla^2\rho=2G+V$ (atomic units, $G>0$ kinetic and $V<0$ potential energy density), so a
negative Laplacian marks regions where the potential energy dominates (shared, covalent
interactions) and a positive one regions where the kinetic energy dominates (closed-shell, ionic or
van der Waals interactions)~\cite{bader,baderessen}. For a pseudo-density this reading is qualitative.

\desc{lnf}{lnf}{dimensionless\quad$[0,1]$}
\Def The fraction of the cell \emph{volume} in which the charge is locally concentrated.
\Frm
\[
\mathrm{lnf}=\frac{1}{N_v}\sumk \mathbf 1\!\left[\nabla^2\rho_k<0\right].
\]
\Phy How much of the space is occupied by concentrated charge (bonds, lone pairs, valence shells)
rather than depleted charge. A uniform electron gas has no curvature at all (flagged value 0).
\Cav Every voxel counts equally, including nearly empty ones, where the sign of a tiny Laplacian is
decided by round-off. This makes $\mathrm{lnf}$ noisier than the charge-weighted version below.

\desc{lnf_charge_weighted}{lnf_charge_weighted}{dimensionless\quad$[0,1]$}
\Def The fraction of the \emph{electrons} that sit where the charge is locally concentrated.
\Frm
\[
\mathrm{lnf}_\rho=\frac{\sum_{k:\,\nabla^2\rho_k<0}\rho_k}{\sumk\rho_k}.
\]
\Phy The share of the charge in concentration regions (pseudo-cores, bonds, lone pairs) as opposed
to the depleted regions around them. Low-density voxels carry little weight, so it is far less
sensitive to the grid than $\mathrm{lnf}$, and it is not interchangeable with it: a crystal can have
a small concentrated volume holding most of its charge.

\desc{lap_concentration_valence}{lap_concentration_valence}{dimensionless\quad$[0,1]$}
\Def The share of the absolute Laplacian outside the core spheres ($s_k>a$) that comes from
concentration regions.
\Frm
\[
L_{\rm val}=\frac{\sum_{k:\,s_k>a,\ \nabla^2\rho_k<0}|\nabla^2\rho_k|}{\sum_{k:\,s_k>a}|\nabla^2\rho_k|}
=\frac12-\frac{\int_{\rm out}\nabla^2\rho\,dV}{2\int_{\rm out}|\nabla^2\rho|\,dV}.
\]
\Phy Over the whole cell the same ratio carries no information: on a periodic cell the Laplacian
integrates to zero, $\int\nabla^2\rho\,dV=0$ (the divergence theorem with no boundary; in Fourier
space the $\mathbf G=0$ component of $\nabla^2\rho$ is $-|\mathbf G|^2\rho(\mathbf G)=0$), so the
positive and negative parts have equal weight and the ratio is $1/2$ for every crystal. Outside the
core spheres this no longer holds, and by the divergence theorem
\[
\int_{s>a}\nabla^2\rho\,dV=-\oint_{\text{core surfaces}}\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial n}\,dS ,
\]
with $\partial\rho/\partial n$ the derivative along the outward normal of the core spheres.
$L_{\rm val}$ thus measures the balance of concentration and depletion in the valence region, and it
is tied to how the density behaves at the core surface. If the density still \emph{falls} outward
at the core surface (a decaying valence tail), the outer region is a net depletion region and
$L_{\rm val}<1/2$; this is the common case. If it still \emph{rises} outward (valence charge that
peaks outside the core spheres, charge piled up in bonds or lone pairs away from the atoms),
$L_{\rm val}$ approaches or exceeds $1/2$.
\Lim Uniform density, or no voxel outside the core spheres: $1/2$, flagged.

\subsection{Shape of the bonds: ellipticity}
At a point on a bond the density is a maximum across the bond and a minimum along it: two Hessian
eigenvalues are negative ($\lambda_1\le\lambda_2<0$, curvatures across the bond) and one positive
($\lambda_3$, along it). If the two negative curvatures are equal the charge has a circular cross
section, as in a $\sigma$ bond; if they differ the cross section is elliptical, as in bonds with
$\pi$ character, where charge is spread in one plane~\cite{baderellip}.

\desc{ellip_bond_bounded_avg}{ellip_bond_bounded_avg}{dimensionless\quad$[0,1)$}
\Def The mean flattening of the charge cross section, over the bond-region voxels where at least two
curvatures are negative.
\Frm With $B=\{k\in\text{bond region}:\ \lambda_{2,k}<0\}$ and the usual ellipticity
$\varepsilon=\lambda_1/\lambda_2-1\ge0$,
\[
\bar b=\frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{k\in B}\left(1-\frac{\lambda_{2,k}}{\lambda_{1,k}}\right)
      =\frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{k\in B}\frac{\varepsilon_k}{1+\varepsilon_k}.
\]
\Phy Each voxel contributes 0 when the charge around it is cylindrical ($\lambda_1=\lambda_2$) and
approaches 1 when it is flattened into a sheet ($\lambda_2\to0^-$). Unlike Bader's ellipticity,
which is evaluated only at bond critical points, $\bar b$ averages over the whole bonding region,
so it describes the typical cross-sectional shape of the charge ridges between the atoms: low for
$\sigma$-type, cylindrical bonding, higher where the bonding charge is planar ($\pi$ character,
layered bonding).
\Lim No such voxel: 0, flagged.
\Cav The bounded form is used because $\varepsilon$ itself diverges as $\lambda_2\to0$: the mean of
$\varepsilon$ changed by a median 22\% between FFT and finite-difference derivatives, while $\bar b$
changed by 0.2\%.

\subsection{Weak interactions (NCI)}
The non-covalent-interaction (NCI) analysis of Johnson \emph{et al.}~\cite{nci} looks for regions of
low density where the density also varies slowly. The measure of slow variation is the reduced
density gradient, the dimensionless inhomogeneity that also enters GGA functionals:
\[
s=\frac{|\nabla\rho|}{2(3\pi^2)^{1/3}\rho^{4/3}}=\frac{|\nabla\rho|}{2k_F\,\rho},\qquad k_F=(3\pi^2\rho)^{1/3}.
\]
$s$ is the relative change of the density over a local Fermi wavelength: $0$ in a uniform electron
gas, small where the density is nearly flat, and large in exponentially decaying tails. Between two
closed-shell fragments the density passes through a shallow trough, where $s$ drops towards 0 at low
$\rho$; that is the NCI signature. pydemi's NCI region is
\[
\mathrm{NCI}=\{k:\ s_k<0.5,\ \ \rho_k<0.05\ \text{e/bohr}^3\ (=0.337\ \text{e/\AA}^3)\}.
\]
The sign of the middle Hessian eigenvalue separates the kinds of contact: $\lambda_2<0$ means the
density is accumulated across the contact (an attractive, bonding-like interaction such as a
hydrogen bond), $\lambda_2>0$ that it is depleted, as at the centre of a ring or cage (a
non-bonded, sterically crowded contact).

\desc{f_NCI}{f_NCI}{dimensionless\quad$[0,1]$}
\Def The fraction of the cell volume that belongs to the NCI region (low and slowly varying
density).
\Frm
\[
f_{\rm NCI}=\frac{1}{N_v}\sumk\mathbf 1\!\left[s_k<0.5\ \text{and}\ \rho_k<0.05\ \text{a.u.}\right].
\]
\Phy In molecules this volume marks the weak interactions. In crystals the same criterion is met
above all by the low, nearly flat interstitial density of metals, which looks locally like a
uniform electron gas ($s\approx0$). In the wide empty gaps of molecular and layered crystals the
density decays exponentially, $s$ is large there, and only the thin troughs between molecules
qualify. On the CMS Lab dataset $f_{\rm NCI}$ is indeed much larger for metals than for insulators.
Read it as ``volume of low, free-electron-like density'' and together with $f_{\rm int}$ and the
percolation levels.
\Lim Uniform density: 0, flagged.

\desc{NCI_attractive}{NCI_attractive}{dimensionless\quad$[0,1]$}
\Def The fraction of the NCI region in which $\lambda_2<0$.
\Frm
\[
\frac{\#\{k\in\mathrm{NCI}:\ \lambda_{2,k}<0\}}{\#\{k\in\mathrm{NCI}\}}.
\]
\Phy The share of the weak-interaction volume in which the density is accumulated across the
contact (attractive, bonding-like) rather than depleted (ring and cage centres, steric contacts).
\Lim No NCI voxel: 0, flagged.

\desc{sign_lambda2_rho_mean}{sign_lambda2_rho_mean}{e/bohr$^3$\quad$(-0.05,0.05)$}
\Def The mean of the density, signed by $\lambda_2$, over the NCI region.
\Frm
\[
\overline{\mathrm{sign}(\lambda_2)\rho}=\frac{1}{\#\mathrm{NCI}}\sum_{k\in\mathrm{NCI}}\mathrm{sign}(\lambda_{2,k})\,\rho_k
\qquad(\rho\ \text{in e/bohr}^3).
\]
\Phy It combines the \emph{type} of the weak contacts (the sign) with their \emph{strength}: the
density at a weak contact grows with the strength of the interaction, lowest for dispersion
contacts and higher for hydrogen bonds. Negative values mean the low-density region is dominated by
attractive contacts, positive values by depleted, non-bonded ones.
\Lim No NCI voxel: 0, flagged.

\subsection{Electrostatics: the Hartree potential}
The electrons create an electrostatic potential. Its potential energy for an electron, the Hartree
potential, follows from $\rho$ by one FFT (Poisson's equation in Fourier space),
\[
V(\mathbf G)=\frac{4\pi k_e\,\rho(\mathbf G)}{|\mathbf G|^2}\ \ (\mathbf G\ne0),\qquad V(\mathbf G=0)=0,\qquad k_e=\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}=14.40\ \text{eV\,\AA}.
\]
This is the repulsion an electron feels from the valence electrons only; the nuclei and cores are
not included. It is large where the electrons are dense and small in the voids. The cell average is
set to zero, so only differences matter.

\desc{V_spread}{V_spread}{eV\quad$\ge 0$}
\Def The standard deviation, over the atoms of the cell, of the Hartree potential at the nuclei.
\Frm With $V(\mathbf R_i)$ obtained by exact Fourier interpolation at nucleus $i$ of the $N_a$ atoms,
\[
V_{\rm spread}=\Big[\frac{1}{N_a}\sum_i\big(V(\mathbf R_i)-\bar V\big)^2\Big]^{1/2},\qquad\bar V=\frac1{N_a}\sum_iV(\mathbf R_i).
\]
\Phy How different the electrostatic environments of the atoms are. It is exactly 0 when all atoms
are symmetry-equivalent (most elemental crystals). It grows when the sites differ in how much
electron charge surrounds them, which happens above all after charge transfer: an anion sits in a
dense electron cloud and feels a strong repulsion, a cation that has given up its valence electrons
much less. It is a rough, density-only signature of ionicity and of inequivalent sites.

\desc{V_int_min}{V_int_min}{eV\quad usually $<0$}
\Def The lowest Hartree potential in the interstitial region ($s_k>b$), relative to the cell
average.
\Frm
\[
V_{\rm int,min}=\min_{k:\,s_k>b}V_k,\qquad\textstyle\sum_kV_k=0 .
\]
\Phy The interstitial point where an electron feels the least repulsion from the other electrons,
i.e.\ the most electron-empty part of the space between the atoms, compared with the average.
Large negative values belong to open structures: van der Waals gaps of layered crystals, the space
between chains and molecules, large cages. Values near zero mean a homogeneous density without deep
voids, as in close-packed metals.
\Lim No interstitial voxel: 0, flagged.

\subsection{Density in the bonds}
The neighbours of atom $i$ are its first coordination shell: $j$ is a neighbour if
$|\mathbf R_{ij}|\le1.1\,d_i$, with $d_i$ the distance from $i$ to its nearest neighbour (the 10\%
allows for slightly distorted environments). The density at each bond midpoint
$\tfrac12(\mathbf R_i+\mathbf R_j)$ is obtained by exact Fourier interpolation.

\desc{rho_mid_mean}{rho_mid_mean}{e/\AA$^3$}
\Def The mean electron density at the midpoints of all nearest-neighbour bonds.
\Frm
\[
\bar\rho_{\rm mid}=\frac{1}{N_b}\sum_{(i,j)}\rho\!\left(\tfrac12(\mathbf R_i+\mathbf R_j)\right).
\]
\Phy The midpoint density is a simple stand-in for the density at the bond critical point, which in
the theory of atoms in molecules increases with bond order and bond strength~\cite{bader}: high for
shared, covalent bonds, low for ionic and metallic contacts. (For a homonuclear bond the two points
coincide by symmetry; for a polar bond the critical point shifts towards the electropositive atom.)
Only first-shell bonds are seen: in a molecular crystal these are the strong intramolecular bonds,
so $\bar\rho_{\rm mid}$ is high there even though the crystal is held together by weak forces.
\Lim No bonds found: 0, flagged. With a PAW pseudo-density it can occasionally be negative.

\desc{rho_mid_std}{rho_mid_std}{e/\AA$^3$\quad$\ge 0$}
\Def The standard deviation of the same midpoint densities.
\Frm
\[
\sigma_{\rm mid}=\Big[\frac{1}{N_b}\sum_{(i,j)}\big(\rho_{{\rm mid},ij}-\bar\rho_{\rm mid}\big)^2\Big]^{1/2}.
\]
\Phy How uneven the bonds are. It is 0 when all first-shell bonds are equivalent (e.g.\ diamond,
fcc metals) and large when the crystal mixes strong and weak bonds: different pairs of species,
distorted polyhedra, coexisting covalent and ionic bonding.

\section{Structural descriptors (18)}

\subsection{Connectivity: percolation}
Draw the region where the density exceeds a level $c$, $\{\rho>c\}$, and lower $c$ from the density
maximum. At first the region consists of isolated islands around the atoms. At some level the
islands join into a path that runs through the whole crystal along a lattice direction
$\mathbf a_\alpha$: it connects a cell to its periodic image (a cluster that merely touches two
opposite faces of the cell does not count; it must wind around the periodic cell).

\desc{rho_perc}{rho_perc_a, rho_perc_b, rho_perc_c}{e/\AA$^3$}
\Def The highest density level at which the charge still forms a continuous path through the
crystal along the lattice vector $\mathbf a$, $\mathbf b$ or $\mathbf c$.
\Frm
\[
\rho_{{\rm perc},\alpha}=\max\big\{c:\ \{\rho>c\}\ \text{spans the crystal along}\ \mathbf a_\alpha\big\}
=\max_{\text{paths along }\mathbf a_\alpha}\ \min_{\mathbf r\in\text{path}}\rho(\mathbf r).
\]
Found by bisection over the voxel values; connectivity through shared faces, periodic boundaries
handled by tracking how many times a cluster winds around the cell.
\Phy The second form says it all: of all paths an electron could take through the crystal along
$\mathbf a_\alpha$, take the one whose lowest point (bottleneck) is highest, and report that lowest
density. The bottleneck is a saddle point of the density, a bond-type critical point (two negative
curvatures, one positive). In a metal the valence charge forms a continuous sea and the bottleneck
is high; in ionic and molecular solids every path has to cross a region of very low density, and
$\rho_{\rm perc}$ is close to zero. In a layered crystal the path across the layers must cross the
van der Waals gap, so $\rho_{\rm perc}$ in that direction drops to the gap density while the
in-plane values stay high. This makes the three values, and their spread below, sensitive to the
bonded dimensionality of the structure.
\Cav The labels $a$, $b$, $c$ refer to the cell vectors as written in the CHGCAR. A different
choice of the same lattice (another setting, a permutation of the vectors) relabels or changes the
three values; their minimum, maximum and the anisotropy below are more robust.

\desc{perc_anisotropy}{perc_anisotropy}{dimensionless\quad$[0,3]$}
\Def The spread of the three percolation levels relative to their mean.
\Frm
\[
A_{\rm perc}=\frac{\max_\alpha\rho_{{\rm perc},\alpha}-\min_\alpha\rho_{{\rm perc},\alpha}}{\tfrac13\sum_\alpha\rho_{{\rm perc},\alpha}} .
\]
\Phy Whether the charge connects equally well in all directions. Ideal cases: isotropic 3-D
bonding gives 0; a layered crystal with two equal in-plane levels and an empty gap direction gives
$1/(2/3)=1.5$; a chain crystal connected along one direction only gives 3. Real layered and chain
compounds fall between these values and 0, well separated from 3-D bonded crystals.
\Lim All three levels zero: 0, flagged.

\subsection{Peaks and valleys: the critical-point census}
The critical points of $\rho$ (where $\nabla\rho=0$) are classified by the signs of the Hessian
eigenvalues: maxima (three negative), bond points (two negative), ring points (one negative) and
minima or cage points (none negative)~\cite{bader}. On a periodic cell their numbers obey the Morse
relation $n_{\max}-n_{\rm bond}+n_{\rm ring}-n_{\min}=0$, because the Euler characteristic of the
3-torus is zero~\cite{pendas}. pydemi counts the maxima and minima directly on the grid: a voxel is a
maximum (minimum) if it is higher (lower) than all 26 neighbours; ties are broken by voxel index.
The saddle counts are not descriptors (they changed by 17--20\% on a coarser grid) but their
Euler-sum check is reported in the metadata. Counts are divided by the cell volume.

\desc{n_max}{n_max}{1/\AA$^3$\quad$\ge 0$}
\Def The number of local maxima of the density per unit volume.
\Frm
\[
n_{\max}=\frac{\#\{k:\ \rho_k>\rho_{k'}\ \text{for all 26 neighbours}\ k'\}}{V}.
\]
\Phy In an all-electron density every nucleus is a maximum, so $n_{\max}$ would equal the number
density of atoms. In the pseudo-density the maxima can sit on a shell around the nucleus, in the
bonds or in lone pairs, so $n_{\max}$ is the atom density plus the number of such additional peaks.
Values well above the atom density point to non-spherical, structured valence charge.

\desc{n_min}{n_min}{1/\AA$^3$\quad$\ge 0$}
\Def The number of local minima of the density per unit volume.
\Frm
\[
n_{\min}=\frac{\#\{k:\ \rho_k<\rho_{k'}\ \text{for all 26 neighbours}\ k'\}}{V}.
\]
\Phy The minima are the cage points: the centres of the empty spaces between the atoms, such as the
interstitial holes of close-packed structures, cages of frameworks and the void centres in molecular
crystals. $n_{\min}$ is therefore the number density of distinct voids.

\desc{n_NNM}{n_NNM}{1/\AA$^3$\quad$\ge 0$}
\Def The number of non-nuclear maxima per unit volume: local maxima outside every core sphere.
\Frm
\[
n_{\rm NNM}=\frac{\#\{k\ \text{a maximum}:\ s_k>a\}}{V}.
\]
In a PAW pseudo-density the valence charge of a heavy atom can peak on a shell around the nucleus
rather than at it. A fixed distance would count such a shell maximum as non-nuclear for a large atom;
the core sphere grows with the atom and keeps it on the atom.
\Phy A maximum away from the nuclei means that charge pools in the space between atoms. The
textbook case is an electride, where electrons occupy cavities and act as anions (e.g.\
[Ca$_{24}$Al$_{28}$O$_{64}$]$^{4+}\cdot$4e$^-$ and Ca$_2$N)~\cite{c12a7,ca2n}. In a valence
pseudo-density the descriptor also counts peaks in covalent bonds and lone pairs: the valence charge
of Si, for instance, is well known to peak on the bonds. On the CMS Lab dataset non-nuclear maxima
occur mainly in compounds of the heavier $p$-block elements (Si, Ge, Sn, Sb, Te, Bi, \ldots), so
$n_{\rm NNM}$ is mostly a marker of directional covalent bonding and lone pairs, and only in rare
cases of electrides.

\desc{Q_NNM}{Q_NNM}{dimensionless\quad$[0,1]$}
\Def The fraction of the electrons that belong to the non-nuclear maxima.
\Frm Every voxel is followed uphill, from neighbour to steepest-ascent neighbour (slope $=$ density
difference / distance), until a maximum is reached; the voxels that end at the same maximum form its
basin, the grid analogue of a Bader basin~\cite{henkelman}. Then
\[
Q_{\rm NNM}=\frac{\sum_{k\in\text{basins of the non-nuclear maxima}}\rho_k}{\sumk\rho_k}.
\]
\Phy How much charge the non-nuclear maxima hold. In an electride it is the share of the electrons
that act as anions; for bond and lone-pair maxima it is the charge assigned to the bonds rather than
to the atoms. It is zero for most crystals.

\subsection{The density floor}
\desc{rho_min}{rho_min}{e/\AA$^3$}
\Def The lowest density anywhere in the cell.
\Frm
\[
\rho_{\min}=\min_k\rho_k .
\]
\Phy In an all-electron density this would be the deepest void, the emptiest point of the crystal.
In a CHGCAR it is usually slightly \emph{negative} and lies near a nucleus, inside an augmentation
sphere, where the PAW pseudization can undershoot. Then it reflects the POTCAR and the atoms present
more than the bonding. A positive $\rho_{\min}$ is the density at the most open point of the
interstitial.

\desc{rho_min_ratio}{rho_min_ratio}{dimensionless}
\Def The lowest density relative to the mean density of the cell.
\Frm
\[
\frac{\rho_{\min}}{\langle\rho\rangle},\qquad \langle\rho\rangle=\frac{Q_{\rm tot}}{V}.
\]
\Phy The same information on a scale that does not depend on how many electrons the cell has: 1 for
a uniform density, close to 0 when there are deep, empty voids, negative when the PAW undershoot
dominates.
\Lim Zero mean density: 0, flagged.

\desc{rho_int_mean}{rho_int_mean}{e/\AA$^3$}
\Def The mean density over the interstitial region, the voxels outside every outer sphere.
\Frm
\[
\bar\rho_{\rm int}=\frac{1}{N_{\rm int}}\sum_{k:\,s_k>b}\rho_k .
\]
\Phy How densely the space between the atoms is filled. Where $f_{\rm int}$ gives the
\emph{fraction of the charge} in the interstitial, $\bar\rho_{\rm int}$ gives the \emph{density
level} there. A high level means a well-filled, free-electron-like interstitial (metals); a low one,
nearly empty voids (ionic, molecular and layered crystals). It can be compared with the density of a
uniform electron gas, $\rho=3/(4\pi r_s^3)$.
\Lim No interstitial voxel: 0, flagged.

\subsection{Directionality: the charge-anisotropy tensor}
Averaging the outer product of the gradient with itself over the cell gives a symmetric
$3\times3$ tensor with trace 1, the analogue of the structure tensor of image analysis:
\[
T_{ab}=\frac{\sumk \partial_a\rho_k\,\partial_b\rho_k}{\sumk |\nabla\rho_k|^2},\qquad
\text{eigenvalues } t_1\le t_2\le t_3,\quad t_1+t_2+t_3=1 .
\]
$T$ is weighted by $|\nabla\rho|^2$, so it is dominated by the steep parts of the density near the
atoms. Being a second-rank tensor property of the crystal, it must be invariant under the crystal's
point group (Neumann's principle). Hence: cubic crystals have $t_1=t_2=t_3=1/3$ exactly;
tetragonal, hexagonal and trigonal crystals have two equal eigenvalues; only orthorhombic,
monoclinic and triclinic crystals can have three different ones.

\desc{T_eigenvalues}{T_eigenvalues_t1, T_eigenvalues_t2, T_eigenvalues_t3}{dimensionless\quad$[0,1]$}
\Def The three eigenvalues of $T$, sorted in increasing order. Each is the share of the squared
density gradient that points along one principal direction.
\Frm $T\mathbf e_j=t_j\mathbf e_j$, $t_1\le t_2\le t_3$, $\sum_jt_j=1$.
\Phy If the density varies equally in all directions, all three are $1/3$. A \emph{layered} density,
which varies most strongly across the layers, puts most of the gradient along the stacking axis: one
large eigenvalue ($t_3$) and two small ones. A \emph{chain-like} density, uniform along the chains and
varying across them, gives one small eigenvalue ($t_1$, the chain axis) and two large ones. The three
values together tell which of these shapes the charge has.
\Lim Uniform density: all $1/3$, flagged.

\desc{charge_FA}{charge_FA}{dimensionless\quad$[0,1]$}
\Def The fractional anisotropy of $T$: one number for how far the gradient distribution is from
isotropic, as used for diffusion tensors~\cite{basser}.
\Frm
\[
\mathrm{FA}=\sqrt{\tfrac32}\,\frac{\lVert T-\tfrac13 I\rVert_F}{\lVert T\rVert_F}
=\sqrt{\tfrac32}\,\frac{\sqrt{\sum_j(t_j-\frac13)^2}}{\sqrt{\sum_jt_j^2}} .
\]
\Phy 0 when $T$ is isotropic (any cubic crystal), 1 when every gradient is parallel (a density that
varies along one direction only). For most three-dimensional crystals it is close to zero; it is
larger for strongly layered or chain-like densities and for low-symmetry cells.
\Lim Uniform density: 0, flagged.

\subsection{Information content}
These treat the normalized density, the shape function $\rhot = \rho/N_e$ with
$N_e=\int\rho\,dV$~\cite{parr}, as a probability distribution over the cell: $\rhot(\mathbf r)\,dV$ is
the probability of finding a given electron in $dV$. Negative pseudo-density values are set to 0,
and atomic units are used. The entropy and the disequilibrium are normalized by the cell volume,
which makes them intensive and makes a uniform density the reference point.

Shannon entropy, disequilibrium and complexity depend only on the \emph{distribution of density
values} over the cell (a histogram of $\rho$), not on where the values are: they treat each voxel on
its own.

\desc{shannon_entropy}{shannon_entropy}{dimensionless (nats)\quad$\le 0$}
\Def The Shannon entropy~\cite{shannon} of the shape function, measured from that of a uniform density.
\Frm
\[
S=-\!\int\rhot\ln\rhot\,dV-\ln V=-\int\rhot\,\ln\frac{\rhot}{1/V}\,dV .
\]
\Phy The second form is minus the Kullback--Leibler divergence of $\rhot$ from the uniform
distribution $1/V$: the information gained by knowing where the charge actually is, rather than
assuming it is spread evenly. $S=0$ for a uniform density (free-electron limit) and becomes more
negative the more the charge concentrates in small regions: on compact atoms, in localized states,
in crystals with large empty voids. $e^{S}$ is the fraction of the cell volume that the charge
effectively occupies.

\desc{disequilibrium}{disequilibrium}{dimensionless\quad$\ge 1$}
\Def The volume-normalized integral of the squared shape function, a measure of how far the density
is from uniform.
\Frm
\[
D = V\!\int\rhot^{\,2}\,dV=\frac{\langle\rho^2\rangle}{\langle\rho\rangle^2}=1+\frac{\mathrm{Var}(\rho)}{\langle\rho\rangle^2},
\]
with $\langle\cdot\rangle$ the average over the cell volume.
\Phy $D-1$ is the squared coefficient of variation of the density over the cell. $D=1$ for a
uniform density and grows as the charge piles up in some places and empties others. $1/D$ is the
fraction of the cell an equivalent ``flat-topped'' density would fill (the second-order Rényi
counterpart of $e^{S}$). Concentrated densities (compact atoms, empty voids) have large $D$.

\desc{LMC_complexity}{LMC_complexity}{dimensionless\quad$\ge 1$}
\Def The L\'opez-Ruiz--Mancini--Calbet statistical complexity~\cite{lmc} in its form for
continuous distributions~\cite{catalan}: disequilibrium times the exponential of the entropy.
\Frm
\[
C = D\,e^{S}=\exp\!\big(H_1-H_2\big),\qquad H_1=-\!\int\rhot\ln\rhot\,dV,\quad H_2=-\ln\!\int\rhot^{\,2}\,dV .
\]
\Phy $H_1$ (Shannon) and $H_2$ (Rényi of order 2) are two ways of counting the effective volume
occupied by the charge; since $H_1\ge H_2$, $C\ge1$, with equality exactly when the density is
constant wherever it is nonzero. The ratio of the two effective volumes does not change when the
density is shifted or uniformly stretched or compressed, so $C$ measures the \emph{shape} of the
density distribution independent of its overall scale: how strongly peaked it is compared with a
flat plateau. It balances order (large $D$) against disorder (large $e^{S}$).

\section{Heterogeneity descriptors (18)}\label{sec:het}
The descriptors above describe the whole cell. These ask \emph{how different the atoms are}. Each
voxel is assigned to an atom; by default to its nearest nucleus, which gives Voronoi cells
(smooth Becke partitions~\cite{becke} give fractional weights). With $w_i(k)=1$ if voxel $k$
belongs to atom $i$ and $r_{ik}$, $\hat u_{ik}$ measured from atom $i$, three per-atom quantities are
computed. Each is the per-atom version of a bonding descriptor:
\begin{align*}
m_1^{(i)}&=\frac{\sum_k w_i(k)\rho_k r_{ik}}{\sum_k w_i(k)\rho_k}
&&\text{the radius of atom $i$'s charge cloud (cf.\ \dn{m1});}\\
f_{\rm bond}^{(i)}&=\frac{\sum_{k:\,aR_i<r_{ik}\le bR_i} w_i(k)\rho_k}{\sum_k w_i(k)\rho_k}
&&\text{the share of atom $i$'s charge in its bond region (cf.\ \dn{f_bond});}\\
\zeta^{(i)}&=1-\frac{\sum_k w_i(k)|\nabla\rho_k\cdot\hat u_{ik}|}{\sum_k w_i(k)|\nabla\rho_k|}
&&\text{the asphericity of atom $i$ (cf.\ \dn{zeta}).}
\end{align*}
For $f_{\rm bond}^{(i)}$, under every partition, each voxel goes to the atom with the smallest
$r_{ik}/R_i$: the sphere that puts the voxel in the bond region also names its atom. The bond
regions of the atoms then add up to the bond region of the cell, and the six $f_{\rm bond}$ site
descriptors do not depend on the partition; those of $m_1$ and $\zeta$ do.
Physically, $m_1^{(i)}$ follows the size and charge state of an atom (a cation that has given away
electrons has a compact cloud, an anion a diffuse one), $f_{\rm bond}^{(i)}$ how much of its charge
it puts into bonds, and $\zeta^{(i)}$ how directional its bonding is. Symmetry-equivalent atoms have
identical values, so every spread below comes from atoms that are \emph{not} equivalent.

For each $X\in\{m_1, f_{\rm bond}, \zeta\}$ six statistics over the $N_a$ atoms are reported. Let
$x_e$ be the fraction of the sites occupied by element $e$, $\bar X_e$ the mean over the sites of
element $e$ and $\bar X$ the mean over all sites. All variances and standard deviations are
population ones (divided by the number of sites).

\desc{X_site_std}{m1_site_std, f_bond_site_std, zeta_site_std}{units of $X$\quad$\ge0$}
\Def The standard deviation of the per-atom values over all atoms of the cell.
\Frm $\mathrm{std}_iX^{(i)}=\big[\tfrac1{N_a}\sum_i(X^{(i)}-\bar X)^2\big]^{1/2}$.
\Phy The overall difference between the atoms. For $m_1$: how different the atoms are in size and
charge state (large in strongly ionic compounds of a small cation with a large anion); for
$f_{\rm bond}$: whether some atoms are bonded much more strongly than others; for $\zeta$: whether
some atoms are spherical and others directionally bonded. Zero for crystals with a single
inequivalent site.

\desc{X_site_range}{m1_site_range, f_bond_site_range, zeta_site_range}{units of $X$\quad$\ge0$}
\Def The difference between the largest and the smallest per-atom value.
\Frm $\max_iX^{(i)}-\min_iX^{(i)}$.
\Phy The most extreme contrast between two atoms of the crystal. Unlike the standard deviation it
does not depend on how many atoms of each kind there are, so a single unusual site (a dopant-like
atom, a terminal atom, a site in an unusual coordination) shows up fully.

\desc{X_site_max}{m1_site_max, f_bond_site_max, zeta_site_max}{units of $X$}
\Def The largest per-atom value.
\Frm $\max_iX^{(i)}$.
\Phy The extreme atom on the high side: the largest, most diffuse atom ($m_1$), the atom with the
most charge in its bonds ($f_{\rm bond}$), the most directionally bonded atom ($\zeta$). Properties
governed by the weakest or softest link of a structure can depend on this atom more than on the
average.

\desc{X_site_min}{m1_site_min, f_bond_site_min, zeta_site_min}{units of $X$}
\Def The smallest per-atom value.
\Frm $\min_iX^{(i)}$.
\Phy The extreme atom on the low side: the most compact atom, typically the most strongly charged
cation ($m_1$); the atom with the least charge in its bond region ($f_{\rm bond}$); the most
spherical atom ($\zeta$).

\desc{X_within_element_var}{m1_within_element_var, f_bond_within_element_var, zeta_within_element_var}{(units of $X$)$^2$\quad$\ge0$}
\Def The part of the site-to-site variance that comes from atoms of the \emph{same} element being
different.
\Frm $\sum_e x_e\,\mathrm{Var}_{i\in e}X^{(i)}$.
\Phy Nonzero only when one element occupies inequivalent sites: different coordinations (tetrahedral
and octahedral cations in a spinel), different oxidation states (mixed valence), bridging and
terminal atoms, different layers of a stacking. It is the charge-density signature of
crystallographically distinct sites of one element.
\Lim 0 (flagged) when no element has two or more sites in the cell. This is the value of any
supercell, in which the copies of a site are equivalent, so the primitive and the conventional cell
of rock-salt NaCl both give 0: ``no inequivalent sites of one element''.

\desc{X_between_element_var}{m1_between_element_var, f_bond_between_element_var, zeta_between_element_var}{(units of $X$)$^2$\quad$\ge0$}
\Def The part of the site-to-site variance that comes from different elements behaving differently.
\Frm $\sum_e x_e\,(\bar X_e-\bar X)^2$.
\Phy The site-fraction-weighted variance of the element means. For $m_1$ it measures the size
contrast between the species, which grows with charge transfer (compact cations, diffuse anions);
for $f_{\rm bond}$ and $\zeta$, how differently the species bond. It plays the role that differences
of tabulated atomic properties (radii, electronegativities) play in composition-based descriptors,
but it is measured in the actual crystal.
\Lim 0 (flagged) for a single-element crystal.

\medskip
The last two split the variance exactly, as in a one-way analysis of variance:
\[
\mathrm{Var}_iX^{(i)}=\underbrace{\sum_e x_e\,\mathrm{Var}_{i\in e}X^{(i)}}_{\text{within}}
+\underbrace{\sum_e x_e\,(\bar X_e-\bar X)^2}_{\text{between}} .
\]
So the square of \dn{X_site_std} is divided into a part due to inequivalent sites of one element and
a part due to the chemistry of the different elements.

\subsection*{Not included, and why}\label{sec:dropped}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=0.3ex,topsep=0.3ex]
\item \emph{ELF and local energy densities} ($G$, $V$, $H$): a CHGCAR has no orbitals, so both
  would have to be estimated from the PAW pseudo-density with a density-only approximation of the
  kinetic energy. The results are not the true quantities.
\item \emph{Mean and spread of the unbounded ellipticity $\varepsilon$}: they diverge as
  $\lambda_2\to0$. On the dataset the mean changed by a median 22\% (FD4 vs.\ FFT derivatives)
  and the spread by 67\%; the bounded form above changed by 0.2\%.
\item \emph{Saddle-point counts}: they changed by a median 17--20\% when the grid was coarsened
  to 80\%.
\end{itemize}
